December 31 is one night with several midnights, and the keyboard does not know that.

Auckland rings in first. Its midnight on December 31 is 3 a.m. in Los Angeles and 6 a.m. in New York. London follows, and London's midnight is 7 p.m. the previous evening in New York. Los Angeles rings in last among the big cities: 8 a.m. in London, 9 p.m. in Auckland. The party popper does not carry a time zone. The fireworks do not carry a time zone. The only object on the phone that even tries to say "it is midnight" is a clock face, and it says it about wherever the person holding the phone happens to be standing.

There is still no New Year emoji. The keyboard has a sky explosion, a bottle, two glasses, a party face, a clock face, and a row of number keys. Every one of them was built for something else and borrowed for this night. The fireworks already worked Diwali seven weeks ago and they work Chinese New Year again in February. The sparkles already worked Christmas. The bottle has a separate career that [the champagne guide](/guides/what-does-champagne-emoji-mean) covers at length. The clock is just a clock. What is left is the part that has to be said in words, and every year a few million people send it in pictures instead.

This year the night is Thursday, December 31, and the day after is Friday, January 1. Diwali was Sunday, November 8 — [the Diwali guide](/guides/diwali-emoji-etiquette) has the dates — and it took the fireworks emoji with it. The next time that emoji is busy is Saturday, February 6, 2027, which is Lunar New Year and the Year of the Goat. The fireworks come back in February. That is how you can tell a borrowed object from an owned one: it works once a year, and the calendar keeps the receipts.

## What actually arrives on the screen

| The message looks like            | The sender means                                                     |
| --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "happy new year 🎆"               | The standard closer, sent at 9 p.m. or 12:01 a.m. depending on where |
| "2️⃣0️⃣2️⃣7️⃣"                        | The year spelled in keycaps — the signature of the whole night       |
| "see you in 2027 🥂"              | The toast, aimed at people who will still be awake                   |
| "3... 2... 1... 🥳"               | The countdown, and an announcement that the sender is busy           |
| "12:00 🕛"                        | A claim about where the sender is                                    |
| "happy new year 🥂" to a coworker | A professional closing line with a drink attached                    |
| "happy ny 💋"                     | A kiss, whatever the sender meant by it                              |
| "new year, new me 💪"             | A promise, aimed at January 2                                        |
| "🥳" at 1 a.m. with no words      | A photograph is about to arrive                                      |

Read the object, then the hour, then the relationship. Happy, cheers, and countdown are three different sentences, and so are midnight, midnight plus three hours, and the year the sender thinks it is.

**The fireworks.** [The fireworks](/emoji/fireworks) are a burst over a city, and in December there is almost nothing else they could plausibly mean. That is the whole reason the New Year works as well as it does. But the object has other owners. It is also Diwali, also Lunar New Year, also a stadium, also a wedding, also a score. The [Diwali guide](/guides/diwali-emoji-etiquette) made the point from the other side of the calendar: a 🎆 in a Diwali-week family chat is not a New Year, it is a Diwali, and one emoji cannot be both in the same year. The firecracker is a different object again and belongs to Lunar New Year specifically — a red paper cylinder on a string, which is why [🧨](/emoji/firecracker) in a December text reads as a Lunar New Year text that arrived early. The [sparkler](/emoji/sparkler) is a person holding one, and belongs in the hand of whoever is holding it; a [sparkler and a firework](/combo/sparkler-firework) is the two objects together, which is a New Year's picture wearing a Lunar New Year's clothes.

**The bottle and the toast.** [The champagne bottle](/emoji/champagne) and [the clinking glasses](/emoji/clinking-glasses) are not interchangeable, and the difference is the whole point of the night. The bottle is one bottle. It has a cork in the air and a spray coming out of the top, which is why it drifted into the "cheers to me" pose and then into the brand-account flex that [the champagne guide](/guides/what-does-champagne-emoji-mean) maps in full. The toast is two glasses meeting. It is the only New Year's object that says _we_, and _we_ is the entire social content of December 31. Send the 🥂 to a friend. The 🍾 is the person at the party holding the bottle, which is a different sentence: "look at me, look at what I have." [A champagne toast](/combo/champagne-clink-bottle) stacks the two and lands somewhere in the middle, which is usually right for a group chat and usually wrong for a one-to-one. A [toast with sparkles](/combo/champagne-clink-sparkles) puts the December decorator on top of it, and that is the version that arrives in a chat where nobody agreed to celebrate.

**The clock face.** [🕛](/emoji/clock-face-twelve-oclock) is the only New Year's emoji that states a time. The clock face set runs in half-hour steps from 🕐 to 🕧, and the twelve o'clock face is the one people reach for when they want to say "it's midnight." Two problems with that. First, twelve o'clock is also noon, and nobody means noon. Second, and worse, 🕛 is a claim about the sender's clock, not the recipient's — which is exactly why a 🕛 sent at 9 p.m. on the East Coast of the United States is not a mistake if the sender is in London, and is a strange thing to say if they are not. The only emoji that could carry a region is the globe, and the globe comes in three versions: [globe showing the Americas](/emoji/globe-americas), [globe showing Europe and Africa](/emoji/globe-europe-africa), [globe showing Asia and Australia](/emoji/globe-asia-australia). Three regions, no way to say "wherever you are," and a [globe with meridians](/emoji/globe-meridians) is a schoolroom model of a planet rather than a clock. The clock is the message. It is just never about you.

**The keycaps.** 2️⃣0️⃣2️⃣7️⃣ is the most New-Year-specific string on the keyboard, and it is the one that breaks most often. The digits are [2️⃣](/emoji/keycap-two-emoji), [0️⃣](/emoji/keycap-zero-emoji), and [7️⃣](/emoji/keycap-seven-emoji), each a number inside a little box. Platforms render them badly and inconsistently: some keep the box, some drop it and leave a bare digit with wide spacing around it, some glue the four together and some space them out, so the same string can arrive as "2027" and as "2 0 2 7". The number is the message and the year is the whole point of it. People also write the year wrong on purpose and by accident. A "see you in 2027" carrying a keycap year that still reads 2️⃣0️⃣2️⃣6️⃣ is the common version, because the calendar on the wall still says 2026 and the fingers follow it. A 2️⃣0️⃣2️⃣7️⃣ on December 30 is not wrong either — it is the hopeful version, which is the same sentence in a different register. And a 2️⃣0️⃣2️⃣6️⃣ on January 2 is someone who did not update.

**The partying face.** [🥳](/emoji/partying-face) arrived in 2018 and it is the emoji that says "I am unreachable until sunrise." [The celebration cheat sheet](/guides/celebration-emoji-cheat-sheet-good-news) is the map of who is allowed which celebration emoji, and the rule for 🥳 is that it is a close-friend register. From a coworker, from a manager, from the group chat with your extended family, 🥳 is an invitation to a party you were not at. It is also a status: a bare 🥳 at 1 a.m. is not a greeting, it is a warning that a photo is about to arrive.

**The kiss.** [💋](/emoji/kiss-mark) is the most misread object of the night. The lips are unambiguous and the ambiguity is entirely in the relationship. "Happy new year 💋" from a partner is one sentence. From a friend it is a joke. From a coworker it is a complaint, or worse. From an ex at midnight it is a decision. [The flirty emoji guide](/guides/flirty-emojis-and-how-to-tell-intent) is the longer map for reading intent in a single picture; the midnight version is simpler, because midnight removes the usual excuse of a joke. If you are not sure which sentence they meant, do not send one back. A [kiss mark and heart](/combo/kiss-mark-heart) doubles down on the ambiguity instead of resolving it.

**The sparkles.** ✨ is the December decorator and it is already spent. [The sparkles guide](/guides/what-does-sparkles-emoji-mean) is why: they are decoration with no object attached, they make any sentence look special, and they never say which occasion they are for. They work in Christmas because Christmas has a dozen other objects to sit next to. On New Year's Eve they are the December leftover, and a "happy new year ✨" reads as a template that was not updated from the week before. [Fireworks and sparkles](/combo/fireworks-sparkles) is the version that at least commits to the burst.

**The party popper and the confetti ball.** [🎉](/emoji/party-popper) and [🎊](/emoji/confetti-ball) say "a party" without saying which one. The [Christmas guide](/guides/christmas-emoji-etiquette) made the point from December: the 🎉 survives Halloween, Christmas, and New Year's, which is exactly the problem — it does not do New Year work, it does party work, and it is the default emoji in every birthday, engagement, promotion, and graduation thread of the year. The confetti ball is a shade more ceremonial, closer to "it is official," and a [balloon party](/combo/balloon-party) or a [cake party](/combo/cake-partying) is a birthday's furniture, not New Year's.

**The fire, the money, and the resolution.** 🔥 and 💰 are cheap. Fire is "the year was lit" and also "that year was a disaster," and [the fire guide](/guides/what-does-fire-emoji-mean) covers how thoroughly the compliment reading took over. Money is "the year is going to pay" and also a brand flex. The resolution emoji have their own momentum: [💪](/emoji/flexed-biceps), [🥗](/emoji/salad), [🎯](/emoji/bullseye), [the money bag](/emoji/money-bag). They are a genre of their own, the January 1 version of the gym photo, and they are the least New Year's-specific pictures on the keyboard. The bullseye is a goal, the money bag is a raise, the salad is a diet that starts Monday.

**The new moon.** [🌑](/emoji/new-moon) in a new year's text is a picture of the moon, and the moon has its own calendar. The lunar cycle runs about 29 and a half days, so the new moon lands on the first of a month only rarely, and in 2027 the moon is actually new on Thursday, January 7 — six days into the year you have already started. The [full moon](/emoji/full-moon) is a December 31 event only by accident. The 🌑 is decoration that sounds like a beginning and is really an astronomical phase, and it is worth knowing that it does not agree with January 1.

## Where December 31 flips

1. **The fireworks at 9 p.m.** Not a mistake on its own — it is the London and Sydney contingency. It is a mistake in a chat where everyone is in the same zone, and a bigger one in a work channel, where a 9 p.m. 🎆 says "I am celebrating" to people who are working until 5. If you are early, say why. "Celebrating in London tonight 🎆" costs one sentence and removes the question.
2. **"Happy New Year" to the wrong midnight.** The phrase is now attached to December 31 by habit, and half the world's midnights happen on a different clock. The greeting still lands; the picture that explains the hour does not. A 🕛 claims the sender's midnight, not yours, and the reader has to do the arithmetic themselves.
3. **The year in the keycaps is off by one.** A "see you in 2027" carrying a 2️⃣0️⃣2️⃣6️⃣ is the classic one: the sentence means next year and the digits mean this one. A 2️⃣0️⃣2️⃣6️⃣ on January 2 is a copy-paste. A 2️⃣0️⃣2️⃣7️⃣ on December 30 is optimism, which is allowed. Match the year the sender used rather than correcting it — the year is the least interesting thing about the text.
4. **The kiss.** 💋 from the wrong person at midnight is the single most misread picture of the night, because midnight removes the plausible deniability of a joke. If it was not obvious to you, it is not obvious to them either.
5. **The party face in a channel you are not in.** 🥳 in a work Slack channel, in a family group chat with relatives you barely speak to, or in a group with your boss reads as a boast, not an invitation. Keep it for the thread where the photos are actually going.
6. **"See you next year 👋🎉."** In a friendly thread it is a New Year's line. In a work thread, next to someone who had a rough year, it reads as a death joke, because "see you next year" has a second and older meaning. Say "see you in 2027" and leave the waving hand out.
7. **The toast to someone who is not drinking.** Dry January is a real and widely announced thing, and a 🥂 aimed at someone who told you they were done for the month is a tone problem, not a spelling problem. [The droplet](/emoji/droplet) and [the cup with a straw](/emoji/cup-straw) exist for the other side of the month.
8. **The borrowed holiday.** A 🎆 sent in a Diwali-week family chat is a Diwali. A 🧨 sent in December is a Lunar New Year that arrived three months early. The fireworks do not move for you, and the calendar is keeping score.
9. **The person who is not celebrating.** Somebody's year ended in an emergency room, a divorce, a layoff, a funeral, a diagnosis. A 🎆 to that person is a sentence with the wrong verb. Some people are not doing New Year, and saying so is a normal thing to say and does not require an explanation. A sentence can ask; a picture cannot.
10. **The resolution on January 2.** "New year, new me 💪" on December 31 is a bit. On January 2 it is a reminder that nothing has changed yet, delivered to the person who sent it first. [The sorry guide](/guides/apology-emojis-when-they-work-and-when-they-read-cheap) is the map for the reverse version of this, when the year starts with an apology.
11. **The late text.** A "happy new year 🎆" on January 5 is not a greeting, it is a delayed one, and it lands on a chat that has already moved on to something else. January 1 is the last day it is a greeting. January 2 is a late greeting. Anything after that is a text about a text.

## Replies that stay with the night they named

- **They sent the fireworks at midnight.** One picture back, or none. A sentence is better than a second celebration emoji, because a second celebration emoji says you were also celebrating, which may be true and may not be what you want on the record.
- **They sent a countdown.** "3... 2... 1..." deserves a 🥂 and a check on the time, not a paragraph. The countdown is a shared ritual, and a paragraph breaks it.
- **They sent the year in keycaps.** Match the year they sent, not the year you expect. If the digits say 2026 inside a "see you next year" text, they were reading the calendar, not thinking about the year turning. Correcting them at midnight is a strange thing to do.
- **They sent the bottle.** 🍾 to a friend is a celebration. 🍾 to a coworker is a brand account with a pulse. [A champagne toast](/combo/champagne-clink-bottle) is the safe middle for a group.
- **They sent 💋.** If you are not sure, do not send one back. Say "happy new year" and let the sentence carry it. Sending [a kiss mark with a heart](/combo/kiss-mark-heart) back answers a question they did not ask.
- **They sent a photo at 1 a.m.** Reply in the morning. The photograph is not a question, and a reply at 1 a.m. starts a second thread nobody wanted.
- **They are in another time zone.** If they are celebrating before you, send the picture anyway. The clock on your phone is the only one that matters to you, and so is the hour you are actually awake for.
- **They are not celebrating.** Ask, in words. "Not doing new year this year" is a normal sentence, and it deserves a normal sentence back — not a 🎆, and not an interrogation.
- **Nothing has been said and it is January 2.** A New Year's text is not owed. Silence is a complete reply, and it is the correct one when you already said it on the 31st.

## FAQ

**Is there a New Year emoji?**

No. There is no New Year emoji on the keyboard this week. The fireworks, the bottle, the toast, the party face, the clock face, and the keycap digits all stand in for it, and each of them already means something else on its own. There is a [red envelope](/emoji/red-envelope) for Lunar New Year, a [lamp](/emoji/diya-lamp) for Diwali, and a [menorah](/emoji/star-david) for Hanukkah — but the Gregorian new year, which is the one half the world rings in on December 31, has no object of its own.

**What do you text at midnight?**

One sentence and at most one picture. "Happy new year 🥂" is the whole text and it is enough. The countdown, the keycap year, and the fireworks are all optional, and a picture stacked on a picture stacked on a sentence is the version that takes someone else five seconds to decode. If you are sending to a group chat, the picture should be the one that at least tries to mean New Year, which today is the keycaps or the clock face, and even those are doing a job for something else.

**Is it rude to send the fireworks before midnight?**

Not on its own, and not when you say why. A 9 p.m. 🎆 with "London's already gone 🎆" is a time zone, not a gaffe. A 9 p.m. 🎆 with no explanation into a chat where everyone is local is a message about the sender's relationship with the calendar. Send it when you mean it, and put the place in the sentence.

**What emoji says "happy new year" without any other context?**

The clock face, 🕛, is the closest thing: it is the only New Year's picture that states a time, and "twelve o'clock" is the year turning. Everything else — the fireworks, the bottle, the toast, the popper — is a celebration that could be any celebration. The problem with 🕛 is that it describes the sender's midnight rather than the recipient's, which is why the sentence is still doing the real work.

**What if I am not celebrating?**

Do not send a picture. "Happy new year" is a fine thing to wish someone who is not celebrating, and so is silence. What does not work is a stack of celebration emoji aimed at somebody whose year was not a good one — the picture makes your cheerfulness the loudest thing in the thread. If you know they had a hard year, a sentence beats a burst every time.

**How late is too late for a New Year's text?**

January 1. A "happy new year" on January 1 is late but alive; it is the text people send when they slept through the countdown and feel slightly guilty about it. On January 2 the chat has moved on and the text is about the text. If you are sending on January 2, say why — "I am late, but" — and skip the fireworks.

Before you send a picture, name the object out loud. A burst is a burst. A bottle is a bottle, and sometimes it is a flex. A clock is a clock, and it is somebody's clock. The midnight is local, the year does not turn everywhere at once, and the sentence is the only part of the text that can say which New Year you mean.
