The first Hanukkah text of 2026 has a deadline that is not midnight. Friday, December 4, is the first night and the start of Shabbat. The lamp is lit before the Shabbat candles, and the Shabbat candles are lit before sunset. A "happy hanukkah" that lands at 7pm is a note to a house that already did the part you were trying to join.

The keyboard has had a menorah since 2015. It still has no dreidel, no latke, and no jelly doughnut. The game and the food go out as a six-sided die, a raw potato, a syrup stack, and the sprinkled ring people send with coffee. The lamp is the one picture that actually names the holiday. Most of the others name a different errand.

This year the count is awkward because the week opens and closes on Shabbat. Sundown on Friday, December 4, through nightfall on Saturday, December 12. Eight lightings. Two of them happen before sunset, not after dark.

## The key that arrived in 2015

Unicode added the menorah in 2015, version 8.0, codepoint U+1F54E. The character name is menorah with nine branches. The names list also calls it a hanukkiah: eight flames for the eight nights, plus the shamash, the helper flame that lights the others and is not part of the count. That is the Hanukkah lamp. It is a different object from the seven-branch lamp of the Temple, and the standard bothered to say so.

The synagogue came in the same batch. The Star of David was already on phones. So was the dotted six-pointed star, the one with a dot in the middle, which had already been a character for years. People reach for the star they already know. The menorah is a few scrolls away, and a family chat that learned its holiday keys in 2014 never went looking.

What the phone draws is a smaller problem than which character you picked. On some keyboards the menorah is a little white symbol on a purple key. On others it is closer to a lamp. Either drawing is one picture of the whole hanukkiah. Neither drawing includes tonight's count. The sentence has to, or the date has to.

The menorah is the greeting. One means hello. A row means the window, or the big public lamp in a plaza. [The synagogue](/emoji/synagogue) is a building. It belongs in "the lighting is downtown at 6, door on the left." It does not belong under "happy hanukkah" with nothing else attached, because a building is not a night.

## Two Fridays, and a week that is easy to miscount

| When in 2026                         | What the text is walking into                                                                                               |
| ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Friday, Dec. 4, before sunset        | Night 1. One flame, lit before the Shabbat candles. The first lighting of the year adds a blessing the later nights do not. |
| Saturday, Dec. 5, after Shabbat ends | Night 2. Two flames. Not during Saturday lunch, and not while it is still Shabbat.                                          |
| Sunday, Dec. 6, at nightfall         | Night 3.                                                                                                                    |
| Monday, Dec. 7                       | Night 4.                                                                                                                    |
| Tuesday, Dec. 8                      | Night 5.                                                                                                                    |
| Wednesday, Dec. 9                    | Night 6.                                                                                                                    |
| Thursday, Dec. 10                    | Night 7.                                                                                                                    |
| Friday, Dec. 11, before sunset       | Night 8. The last lighting. Eight flames, again before the Shabbat candles.                                                 |
| Saturday, Dec. 12                    | The eighth day. Nothing new is lit after dark. The holiday ends at nightfall.                                               |
| Sunday, Dec. 13                      | Late.                                                                                                                       |

A Thursday text on December 3 is early on purpose, the same way you write "see you tomorrow." Monday morning, December 7, is still inside the week. That evening is night 4. It is not a second opening, and it does not need a fresh explanation of the holiday.

In early December, sunset falls inside the workday in a lot of American and European cities. A 5pm Friday meeting can already be too late for someone lighting at home. The emoji in the status channel will not move the meeting. If you manage that person, December 4 and December 11 are the two afternoons that matter.

Spell it the way that house spells it. Hanukkah, Chanukah, Chanuka, and Hanukah are all English spellings of Hebrew חנוכה. Correcting the spelling inside a greeting is a strange kind of care. If you do not know their spelling, "thinking of you for the lighting 🕎" does not need one. If they wrote "chag sameach," send that back. It means happy holiday. You do not have to upgrade it into a fuller Hebrew sentence you looked up.

December 4 through December 12 also sits in the middle of Christmas season. The family chat may already be full of trees. Answer the holiday they named. [The Christmas guide](/guides/christmas-emoji-etiquette) is the tree. A menorah does not stand in for a tree in a house that keeps Christmas, and a tree does not stand in for the lamp in a house that does not. A mixed household can want both. Those are still two messages.

Shabbat observers may not answer on Friday night or during Saturday. The missing reply is not a snub, and a second menorah sent to bump the thread is not a nudge. It is a ping during a day they already stepped away from the phone. Saturday night, after Shabbat ends, is when night 2 actually starts. That is the next honest moment to write, if you missed Friday.

## Pictures that are not the lamp

A few messages show up every year. The words around them decide more than the glow does.

- "lighting before sunset, shabbat too 🕎" is logistics. They are telling you the deadline.
- "happy chanukah 🎄" from an office account is a Christmas card with one word swapped.
- "jelly doughnuts are in the car 🍩" is food. Believe the food.
- "night 4, come if you want 🕎" is an invitation to one lighting, not a new holiday.
- "missed friday, here for the rest of the week 🕎" on December 6 is on time.
- "the plaza one is at 6 🕍" is an address.
- "chocolate coins for the winner 🪙" is candy. A bare coin, in a chat that talks about prices, is money.

**The star.** [The Star of David](/emoji/star-david) is ✡️, a mark of Judaism in every month, not a counter for the night. A cousin who puts it on every family text is being that cousin. An office account that searched "Jewish emoji" and stopped there has sent a label. The dotted star, 🔯, is a different character. Keyboard search files it under fortune and under Jewish, and the dot in the middle is what separates it from ✡️. People send it when they meant the Star of David and hit the neighbor. On this week it is still not the lamp. Set beside the real star, it looks like the search results got pasted in.

**The wax candle.** [The candle](/emoji/candle) already means a memorial in a lot of threads, which is why [the condolence guide](/guides/condolence-emoji-etiquette) treats a bare candle as a vigil. "happy hanukkah 🕯️" can land as that vigil in a hard year, or as a birthday wish if the thread was just on cake. [The birthday candle combo](/combo/birthday-cake-candle) is the wish. Hanukkah candles are a count that goes up. One wax candle does not count. If you mean the lighting, say so, and use the menorah.

**The breakfast doughnut.** Sufganiyot are filled, fried, and central in a lot of Israeli and Ashkenazi kitchens. The emoji is a ring with sprinkles. [The doughnut](/emoji/donut) plus "jelly doughnuts" is legible. [Doughnut and coffee](/combo/donut-coffee) is a weekday morning. Without the sentence, that pair will not become the holiday.

**The pancake stack.** Latkes are the potato pancake American cards assume. They are not every kitchen. Other tables fry bimuelos, sfenj, or something that house has always fried, because the story people are feeding is about oil. Some kitchens lean dairy instead. [Pancakes](/emoji/pancakes) are a syrup stack. [Pancakes and a maple leaf](/combo/pancakes-maple) are brunch, often a Canada joke. [A potato](/emoji/potato) is raw. If the box on the counter is the point, type "latkes," or type the food they actually make.

**The olive and the coin.** The story people retell is about oil lasting. [The olive](/emoji/olive) is the fruit. It reads as a garnish or a martini. Chocolate gelt is a game token. [The coin](/emoji/coin) showed up years later, and in group chats it often means cash or crypto. "chocolate coins 🪙" works. A coin with no noun looks like a tip.

**The die.** There is no dreidel emoji. The characters added in 2026 did not include one. A dreidel has four sides and Hebrew letters. [The game die](/emoji/game-die) is a cube with five on top, the tabletop die. [The die and a clover](/combo/game-die-lucky) is a luck roll, the wish you send before the dice land. Send it when you are actually rolling dice. It is a confusing way to say the kids have the dreidel out.

**The present.** Plenty of North American family chats do gifts, one a night or one at the end, because the school calendar puts this week next to Christmas. That practice is real in those houses. It is not the lighting. [A wrapped gift](/emoji/wrapped-gift) with no words is also a birthday and also Christmas morning. [The party popper](/emoji/party-popper) is every party, which is why it lives on [the celebration cheat sheet](/guides/celebration-emoji-cheat-sheet-good-news) and not on this one.

**The flag.** [The flag of Israel](/emoji/flag-israel) is a country. A family that already flies it will know why it is in their chat. Someone outside that chat, using the flag as the entire Hanukkah greeting, is talking about a state. The lamp is the holiday. If you are not sure the flag is welcome in that thread, leave it out.

**Blue, white, sparkles, fire.** [A blue heart](/emoji/blue-heart) and [a white heart](/emoji/white-heart) are a palette. White sitting next to a candle drifts toward the memorial reading. [Sparkles](/emoji/sparkles) are the generic card. The longer version is [the sparkles guide](/guides/what-does-sparkles-emoji-mean). [Fireworks and sparkles](/combo/fireworks-sparkles) are a countdown or a launch. [Fire](/emoji/fire) is a compliment or a warning, which [the fire guide](/guides/what-does-fire-emoji-mean) already covers. None of those is a lamp.

One more flame is easy to mix in if you are sending cards to everyone. [Diwali](/guides/diwali-emoji-etiquette) was early November, and that night has its own lamp. A diya and a menorah are both small flames. They are not the same week, and a text that bundles "all the festivals of light" is a search result with a send button.

## Where the text misses the lighting

1. **7pm on a Friday.** December 4 and December 11. If they lit before sunset, your evening menorah missed the lighting. If they keep Shabbat, you may also have missed the window in which they will see a phone. Wait until Saturday night.
2. **The explainer.** "Happy Hanukkah 🕎, festival of lights, eight nights, miracle of oil ✡️🔯" teaches a person's holiday back to them. They have the holiday. The dotted star is the symbol that does not belong in that row.
3. **The tree, sent to be inclusive.** "happy holidays 🎄🕎" handed to a household that does not keep Christmas puts a tree on their greeting. If you know they want both, send two thoughts. If you know they want one, send that one.
4. **The guess.** A menorah "just in case," to a colleague whose family you do not know, is a guess about their religion. Wait for their lamp, or write a sentence with no picture.
5. **A hard year.** If the house is sitting shiva, or they have said they are skipping the holiday, a festive stack is the wrong register. A lone candle can read as the vigil. Ask with words. [The condolence guide](/guides/condolence-emoji-etiquette) is the candle's other job.
6. **Eight posts.** One greeting from a person is the normal amount. A sibling can talk about the food every night. A brand, or a manager, sending eight scheduled menorahs is a campaign. The campaign is the part people mute.
7. **The grocery you assumed.** 🥔🥞 sent to a family that does not make latkes announces where you learned the holiday. Name their food, or skip the food emoji.
8. **The spelling lesson.** If they wrote "Hanukah," they can read their own greeting. An emoji will not recite the blessings either. Night 1 has an extra one. The later nights do not. If they wanted the words, they would not ask a picture.

## What to send back

- **They sent 🕎.** One menorah back, and the word they used. Do not add a star, a flag, and a doughnut so the reply looks complete.
- **They sent only the star, and you know they mean this week.** Answer with the menorah, so the thread gets the object. Matching ✡️ and nothing else teaches the chat that the star was the whole holiday.
- **They are talking about food or the dreidel.** Stay on that. "save me a jelly one 🍩" is a doughnut on purpose. It does not also have to be a greeting.
- **They covered your Friday, or they hosted.** The thanks belongs in the sentence. [Grateful thanks](/combo/pray-thanks) is folded hands plus sparkles: real thanks, plus the same generic shine cards use. [Folded hands](/emoji/folded-hands) alone already splits by phone. [The folded-hands guide](/guides/what-does-folded-hands-mean) is that split. On this week, write the thanks out.
- **You missed Friday.** Saturday night is night 2, not a failure. "Missed the first lighting, here for the rest 🕎" is on time through December 12. A text on December 20 is an apology for a late card. Send the apology, not a pretend greeting.
- **You are not sure they mark it.** No picture.

The useful question before you hit send is which Friday you are in, and which object you actually picked. Night 1 and night 8 are before sunset. The nights between them are after dark, and the Saturday in the middle waits until Shabbat is over. A menorah can carry that week. A tree, a die, a sprinkled ring, and a dotted star are busy being other things.

## FAQ

**What emoji do you send for Hanukkah?**

🕎, with the words. One menorah is the greeting. The Star of David is a year-round mark of Judaism, not the night count. A Christmas tree, a party popper, sparkles, and a single wax candle belong to other messages.

**When should the 2026 text go out?**

The first lighting is Friday, December 4, before sunset and before the Shabbat candles. The last lighting is Friday, December 11, with the same rule. The holiday ends at nightfall on Saturday, December 12. December 13 is late. For most people, one greeting is the whole job. You do not owe eight.

**Why are there two Friday deadlines?**

In 2026 the holiday starts on a Friday, and the eighth night falls on the next Friday. Both of those lightings happen before Shabbat starts. Saturday, December 5, is night 2, and that lighting waits until Shabbat ends. Saturday, December 12, is the last day, and nothing new is lit that night.

**Is the doughnut emoji wrong?**

The sprinkled ring is the wrong pastry, and beside a coffee cup it is breakfast. People still use 🍩 for sufganiyot. The sentence has to say filled, jelly, or the holiday. [Doughnut and coffee](/combo/donut-coffee) will not say it for you.

**Someone sent the dotted star. What is that doing there?**

🔯 is not ✡️. The dotted six-pointed star is a separate character, tagged for fortune as well as Jewish, and the dot is the difference. Answer the greeting they were aiming at. Do not add the dotted star to look thorough.
