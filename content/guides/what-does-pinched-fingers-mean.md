Two people can send the same 🤌 and mean opposite things, and neither of them finds out until the reply comes back.

The drawing was standardized from an Italian gesture: pinched fingertips flicked open toward you, the one Italians use to say _ma che vuoi_, what do you want? Read by an English speaker as a chef kissing their fingertips, that same shape says something close to the opposite. Approval went viral. The exasperation stayed where it started.

Both readings now live on one key, and the picture you send does not tell the other person which one you picked.

This is the story of an emoji that got one meaning from its designers and a different one from its users, and how to read it without misfiring.

## The gesture Unicode picked

The official name is "Pinched Fingers." The codepoint is U+1F90C, approved in Unicode 13.0 in 2020, and the artwork is a hand with the thumb and fingertips pinched together and the other fingers raised. That is the whole specification. Unicode ships a picture, a name, and a keyword for translation. It does not ship a meaning, and this one arrived from two directions at once.

Its source is visible in the artwork. Italian gesticulation has a large vocabulary of single-hand gestures, and the pinched-fingertips one is among the most recognizable: bring the tips together, point them upward, shake or flick the hand open toward whoever is talking. Depending on the tone it lands as _ma che vuoi_ (what do you want?), _ma che stai dicendo_ (what are you even saying?), or a theatrical _davvero?_ (really?). Disbelief, irritation, and performed exasperation rolled into one hand.

The gesture is not exclusively Italian. That same pinched shape turns up across the Mediterranean, and the meanings travel with it. In Israeli usage, pinched fingers can mean hold on, relax, be patient. One hand, different sentence, different country. The Unicode drawing picked the Italian version, which is where the trouble starts.

Then the accident. Held still, a pinched hand with the other fingers raised looks exactly like a chef's kiss, and to anyone who has never watched an Italian wave with both hands, it looks like the kitchen and not the country.

## The reading that went viral

A chef who tastes something perfect brings their fingertips to their lips. "Chef's kiss" entered English as a borrowed intensifier meaning flawless, exactly right, nothing more to add. The gesture has a name and an [🧑‍🍳 emoji of its own](/emoji/chef). What matters here is that the phrase has no object: the meal is chef's kiss, the dress code is chef's kiss, the comeback is chef's kiss.

The internet picked this reading for a reason. Approval works on anything. A meal, a design, a sentence, a sunset, a dog. The Italian version only works on things that annoyed you, which is a much smaller set of moments to have a reaction emoji for. Approval is also the safer of the two to send to someone you do not know. "What do you want?" aimed at a stranger is a small insult, while "this is perfect" is a gift.

TikTok then did something interesting to both readings. The platform built its reach on the approval version, and the emoji now sits in a lot of food and reaction content, the chef's kiss with the fingers pinched and the approval plainly written across the face of the poster. At the same time it gave the Italian version a second life as content of its own. Explainers of _ma che vuoi_, "things only Italians understand" videos, POV skits about someone who only knows Italian hand gestures. The same audience learned both readings at once, one as the default and one as the bit.

Fandom gave it a third. K-pop idol Yuri has used 🤌 as a signature gesture attached to a dumpling, and in that context it is a personal sign-off rather than a general-purpose reaction. When an emoji acquires a fandom meaning it almost always attaches to a person who uses it the same way every time, not to the standard.

What you ended up with is an emoji with a default reading and a specialist one. Almost everyone receiving 🤌 hears approval. A much smaller group, mostly Italians and people who grew up on Italian gesture videos, hears a shrug that points at the other person and asks them to explain themselves.

## What 🤌 is doing in a message

| The message looks like                    | The sender is usually reaching for        | What most recipients hear    |
| ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| "this place is unreal 🤌"                 | perfect, worth the drive                  | chef's kiss                  |
| "the way she answered him 🤌"             | that was absurd and I loved it            | approval, or a laugh         |
| "why is the brief still in this shape 🤌" | I am done asking                          | what do you want?            |
| "you texted me at 2am 🤌"                 | I am genuinely annoyed                    | huh?                         |
| "andiamo 🤌" from an Italian friend       | hello, said the most Italian way possible | a costume                    |
| "hold on, I'm coming 🤌" from an Israeli  | be patient                                | confusion, almost everywhere |

The pattern: the sender picks a gesture, the recipient picks a reading, and the two rarely line up. A few clusters show up again and again.

- **Approval.** The dominant reading, and the safe default for strangers. "this was incredible 🤌", "she said exactly what I needed 🤌".
- **Exasperation.** Needs a target, because it is aimed at somebody. "and you chose this 🤌", "again? 🤌".
- **Performed Italian-ness.** "look at me 🤌" from a non-Italian is affectionate most of the time and a caricature the rest of the time. Italian speakers are not always charmed by it.
- **A full stop.** Often the entire message, doing the work of a period or a "haha."
- **A fandom sign-off.** Inside a specific fandom, 🤌 can be a personal logo. It means nothing outside that fandom, which is fine.

## Where 🤌 flips on you

1. **In a thread where somebody is hurting.** "I got passed over for the role 🤌" answered with 🤌 reads as approval of their pain. If your point is _are you serious_, write the sentence. The emoji will be heard as a compliment by most of the room.
2. **When you actually mean the Italian version.** This is the commonest way 🤌 misfires, and it is the embarrassing one, because the two readings are opposites. You send the flick and they hear the kiss. Write "what do you want?" in words if that is what you mean.
3. **In a thread about somebody's work.** A 🤌 under a colleague's proposal is a pat on the back at best. At worst it is the silent version of _I did not ask for this_. Reviews want sentences.
4. **When nothing about the moment is light.** A hospital update, a break-up, a funeral, a layoff call. 🤌 has no weight setting. It cannot be dialed down.
5. **The 👌 collision.** 👌 sits one tap away in every picker and does a different job. It means approval and "got it," and in some corners it is not an approval at all. The [OK Hand page](/emoji/ok-hand) covers that gap in more detail. Before sending, check which one you actually grabbed.
6. **The costume problem.** Sending 🤌 to an Italian is borrowing a gesture from their country. Once is a joke you are both in. Twice is a bit at their expense. The same applies to speakers of any culture with a pinched-finger gesture of its own, each carrying a meaning you are borrowing without knowing it.
7. **The first message.** A cold 🤌 with no thread underneath it reads as smug regardless of which meaning you meant, because both readings carry a bit of attitude and neither carries warmth.

## Replies that volley back

- **Match it with 🤌.** If the sender is doing approval, the seal is 🤌 back. The [🤌🤌 Italian Pinch combo](/combo/pinched-fingers-italian) is the doubled version, for when the reaction needs to land harder than one glyph can.
- **Match the gesture with the gesture.** If someone sent you the Italian _ma che vuoi_, reply with the flick and no words. You have just confirmed you know the gesture, which is the entire reply.
- **Trade it for food.** When the approval is about a meal, [🍕🍺 Pizza Beer](/combo/pizza-beer) or [🍕✨ Pizza Sparkle](/combo/pizza-slice-sparkles) carry the same warmth without the ambiguity.
- **Turn the approval up.** [🫶💋 Hand Heart Kiss](/combo/hand-heart-kiss-mark) is the chef's kiss with a kiss attached, for when a plain 🤌 is too clinical.
- **Say the sentence.** When the reading matters more than the vibe, "this is perfect, exactly how I wanted it" cannot be misread by anyone. That is the move in any thread where the stakes are higher than a group chat.
- **Swap in the cousin.** [👌✅ Ok Confirm](/combo/ok-hand-check-mark) does the approval job you were reaching for with 🤌, with none of the Italian baggage.

Two more alternatives worth knowing: [😋 the Money-Mouth Face](/emoji/money-mouth-face) is the food-specific version of the same approval, and [🤭 the face with a hand over its mouth](/emoji/face-with-hand-over-mouth) is what the approval read sounds like when you would rather laugh than compliment.

The habit worth building with 🤌 is to notice which hand you are picturing before you send it. Picturing a chef means you are sending a compliment, and almost everybody will receive it as one. Picturing the flick means you are sending a challenge to a room that may hear a compliment instead, and at that point the words are cheaper than the emoji. 🤌 is small enough to fire on autopilot. The two meanings are not small enough to send on autopilot.

## FAQ

**What does 🤌 mean in texting?**

The dominant reading is approval: something is perfect, delicious, exactly right. The emoji was designed from an Italian gesture that points the other way, disbelief and "what do you want?", and that second reading is well known to people who grew up with it.

**Is 🤌 the same as 👌?**

No. 👌 is the OK hand, meaning approval or "got it," and in some contexts something close to a vulgarity. 🤌 is pinched fingertips, which carries the approval, the Italian flick, or the mock Italian bit depending on who is reading. They sit near each other in the picker and get confused constantly.

**Is 🤌 rude?**

It can be. The risk is highest in three places: a thread where somebody is venting, a thread about work, and anywhere the moment is supposed to carry weight. In a group chat with friends it is close to impossible to offend anyone with it.

**Why do Italians use 🤌 so much?**

Because it is their gesture. The Italian _ma che vuoi_ flick is a normal piece of Italian body language, and the emoji is a drawing of it. When an Italian sends it, they are using the gesture it was standardized from, not the joke.

**What does 🤌 mean from a guy or a girl?**

Nothing different. The reading is set by the thread, not the sender. The one thing worth knowing is that 🤌 carries no romantic charge on its own, so it is no substitute for anything warmer.
