Your phone never asked you. You opened the picker, tapped the waving hand, and sent a yellow hand to a person whose hands are not yellow. That default has been running underneath every conversation you have had since emoji learned to wear skin, and it is the least examined decision anywhere on the keyboard.

The swatch in an emoji is a tiny self-portrait. Send 👋🏽 and you are waving as a particular person. Send 👋 and you are waving as nobody in particular, which is a different sentence even though the two drawings look identical at a glance.

Five swatches ship, plus the yellow default, and people read all six. Some treat the yellow as deliberate neutrality. Others treat it as the leftover from a design where every human face was drawn the same golden yellow. Neither camp is imagining it, and which reading lands in your thread depends almost entirely on who is on the other end of it.

The reference page for the waving hand is at [/emoji/hand-wave](/emoji/hand-wave). What it cannot hold is the part that matters in a conversation: what the swatch claims, when a stranger can read it as a statement, and the rules that keep you out of trouble.

## The yellow hand you have been sending since 2018

Unicode 8.0, in June 2015, added five modifier code points. They are named after a dermatological scale and they are still called EMOJI MODIFIER FITZPATRICK TYPE-1-2, TYPE-3, TYPE-4, TYPE-5 and TYPE-6. On their own they did nothing. They became usable emoji in Emoji 11.0 in June 2018, the release that gave the waving hand, the thumbs up and the raised hands their swatches. So your emoji has had a skin for eight years, and the yellow you never picked has been the default since long before that.

The modifiers attach to people and body parts, which is why the [👋 waving hand](/emoji/hand-wave) takes a swatch and the [🎉 party popper](/emoji/party-popper) never will. The list of characters that accept one is long, and it has gaps. The family sequences are one of them. Unicode has never shipped a toned 👨‍👩‍👦, so a whole family stays in the default yellow while the couple-with-heart sitting next to it comes in five shades.

Under the hood the sequences are less tidy than the picker suggests. A plain person plus a swatch is two code points, so 👩🏽 is a woman followed by a modifier and nothing else. Add a job or a hairstyle and the sequence grows a joiner in the middle. Some older phones and some desktop chat clients still render that whole thing as two empty squares, which is why the tone sometimes appears to vanish on the way out.

Some pairs do carry two different swatches at once. Two people holding hands can, so 👩🏻‍🤝‍👩🏿 ships with a light hand and a dark one in a single character. The handshake cannot. 🤝 wears one swatch across both of its hands, so a two-tone handshake has to be faked by sending two of them, which is why 🤝🏻🤝🏼 exists in practice and not in Unicode.

## Five swatches, and what each one is doing

The Fitzpatrick scale was published in 1975 and it measures how skin burns in the sun. It has six types. Unicode ships five swatches, because types 1 and 2 share the light one, and the darkest type gets the single swatch Unicode describes as deeply pigmented dark brown to black.

| Swatch | Modifier | Unicode name           | Fitzpatrick type |
| ------ | -------- | ---------------------- | ---------------- |
| 🏻     | U+1F3FB  | light skin tone        | type 1 and 2     |
| 🏼     | U+1F3FC  | medium-light skin tone | type 3           |
| 🏽     | U+1F3FD  | medium skin tone       | type 4           |
| 🏾     | U+1F3FE  | medium-dark skin tone  | type 5           |
| 🏿     | U+1F3FF  | dark skin tone         | type 6           |

Three consequences follow from that arithmetic, and all three show up in real chats.

**The swatch is an approximation.** Two people with the same skin regularly land on different swatches, and nobody has ever been wrong for picking the closer one. The scale measures sunburn, not identity, and it was never a map of anybody.

**The medium swatch sits close to the default.** On a white screen, 🏽 and the plain yellow are near enough that the choice can be invisible at a glance. That is the usual reason somebody says they picked a tone and nothing changed.

**The darkest swatch is a brown.** People who need a deeper brown than the set contains are the reason the set gets argued about, and no amount of picking fixes it. That is a limit of the artwork, not a limit of the sender.

What the swatch says socially is much simpler than what it technically is. It says the gesture belongs to somebody. A toned 👋 is you waving, not a wave in general. A toned emoji dropped next to a friend's message in a group chat can look like you are speaking as them. And a swatch is skin: it says nothing about ethnicity, nationality or gender, even though people read all three into it. The other attributes come from the base character and from the person sending it.

The yellow default is the swatch that says you did not pick. It carries the least information of the six, which is why it is both the most common and the one most likely to be criticised.

## Where the tone gets read

**At work.** The swatch rarely carries status. What it carries is who the emoji is about. A manager sending "👋🏽 welcome aboard" for a new hire with a different skin tone has picked somebody else's skin for them, and that message lands badly every single time. It is the most reliable way to turn a welcome into a conversation about your office.

**In dating.** Profiles are full of toned emoji, and people do compare them against the photos in the same profile. A mismatch is not a scandal, but it gets noticed, and being noticed is the reason some people deliberately leave their profile emoji yellow.

**In the family chat.** The default yellow from your aunt's phone is not a statement. The oldest devices and the least updated apps still send yellow, and a yellow hand from her means she waved at you.

**Under a stranger's post.** A toned emoji in a public reply makes the comment look written by a person instead of by an account. In a thread where every other reply is toned, the yellow one starts to look automated.

## Six messages and the swatch each one carries

Every emoji that accepts a swatch behaves this way, from the [👍🏾 dark thumbs up](/emoji/thumbs-up-dark) to the [🙌🏽 medium raised hands](/emoji/raising-hands-medium) and the [🫂 hug](/emoji/people-hugging). What changes from message to message is not the words.

- **"good morning 👋🏽"** in a family group chat. Your hand, your morning. The same greeting with the yellow 👋 says hello on behalf of nobody.
- **"👋🏽 welcome to the team!"** in a work channel, for a new hire who is not that shade. You picked their skin for them, and the whole channel can see that you did.
- **"👍🏾 nice work"** under a colleague's deliverable. An approval with a face attached to it, which is warmer than the bare 👍 and heavier than the sentence.
- **"ok 👋🏿"** closing a thread with a friend who always uses the dark swatch. Matching them says you are on the same side of it.
- **"hmm 👋🏻"** replying to a partner's 👋🏿. The words are curious, and the swatch says you are standing a step back from them.
- **"🎉 congrats!"** after somebody's news. The party popper has no skin, so the message claims nothing about the sender.

## The rules nobody published

1. **Pick your own tone, when you want to pick one.** It is a self-portrait, so it is yours to fill in.
2. **Never pick a tone for somebody else.** Not in a welcome message, not in a group announcement, not in a story where they are the subject.
3. **Do not read a yellow emoji as a statement about the sender.** They probably did not think about it at all.
4. **Match their tone when you are speaking as them, keep yours when you are speaking as yourself.** Replying to a partner's 👋🏿 with 👋🏻 is a small way of saying you are standing a step back from them, and people do notice it.
5. **Do not use a swatch as a punchline.** The joke works for the person telling it and lands on everybody else in the thread.
6. **Set the tone once instead of deciding it every time.** Slack has the real setting (Preferences, Appearance, Default emoji skin tone). Discord takes the tone from the OK hand beside the emoji search bar. Twitter and X keep whatever you chose last in the picker. Phones usually stick with the last swatch you long-pressed, though iOS users report the choice reverting now and then.

## Where the swatch misfires

1. **Choosing for somebody else.** The welcome wave aimed at the new hire is the standard example, and it never reads the way the sender meant it.
2. **Reading the yellow as a confession.** The default is a default. Turning it into evidence is the fastest way to start a fight you did not have.
3. **Using the swatch as a joke.** Race jokes land differently depending on who is in the room, and the emoji version lands louder because it is a picture.
4. **Replying in a different tone than the person you are replying to.** It reads as distance, and sometimes as a mistake on your keyboard.
5. **Sending a toned person emoji to an older client.** Two empty squares arrive instead of a face, and the message reads as broken rather than as representational.
6. **Claiming a group with one person.** A single toned hand in a family chat says one person. A swatch cannot represent four cousins.
7. **Changing tone mid-thread.** Sending the plain yellow in the morning and 🏾 after lunch makes people wonder what changed, which is usually not the question you wanted.
8. **Letting the tone carry a rude sentence.** A warmer hand does not soften "you never listen", it just makes the sentence look stranger.
9. **Mixing tones inside a combo.** When one half of a two-emoji sequence wears a swatch and the other half does not, the pair can look like it came from two different conversations.
10. **Reading swatches as ethnicity.** They are skin. People with the same skin tone come from everywhere.
11. **Sending the yellow into a thread where everyone else has a tone.** You read it as carelessness. They read it as a bot.
12. **Assuming the sender picked on purpose.** Most people did not, and most of the time nobody was thinking about it at all.

## Replies that keep the tone out of the sentence

- **Match the swatch when you are answering for somebody.** If you are telling a group what your partner said, their tone keeps the message theirs.
- **Keep your own tone when the message is yours.** A toned emoji is a self-portrait, and it does not need to agree with anybody else's.
- **Use [🙌🎉 the raised-hands celebration](/combo/celebrate-hands) when the news is loud.** The raised hands wear a swatch and the party popper does not, so the combo never contradicts itself.
- **Reach for [🫶❤️ the hand-heart pair](/combo/hand-heart-love) when the warmth is the point.** The hand takes a tone, the heart does not, and the combination stays warm whoever sends it.
- **Try [🫶💋 the hand-heart kiss](/combo/hand-heart-kiss-mark) for encouragement you want to land as care.** One toned hand, one object, no mismatch to read.
- **Keep the work version clean with [🤝💼 the handshake and briefcase](/combo/handshake-briefcase).** The [🤝 handshake](/emoji/handshake) wears one swatch for both hands, so there is no tone question to answer.
- **Let the object carry it.** 🎉, 🎁 and ✨ have no skin at all, which makes them the safest reply when the subject is the sender rather than the message.
- **Say the thing in words.** "Good morning" needs no swatch, and it cannot be misread as a statement about anybody.

## FAQ

**Should I pick a skin tone for my emoji?**
Pick your own if you want the emoji to read as you. That is the whole use. You are not obliged to, and the yellow default is not a mistake.

**Which skin tone should I use?**
The one closest to your own skin. People pick the closest swatch, not an exact match, and nobody else can tell the difference.

**Does the default yellow mean anything about me?**
It means you did not pick. The yellow was drawn as a generic and it is read as neutral by some people and as the residue of a one-colour design by others, which is why it is safer never to assume.

**Why does my emoji show up as two empty squares?**
The person or the client on the other end does not support the modifier, or does not support the joined sequence with a job or a hairstyle attached. Send the plain version and say the rest in words.

**Is it weird if I use a different skin tone than the person I am texting?**
It reads as distance if the message is about them, and it reads as nothing at all if the message is about you. Match their swatch for the first case and keep yours for the second.
