Your client asks whether the launch can move to Thursday. You type "thursday works 🤝" because it takes one tap and it feels warmer than a bare yes. What you have actually done is agree to something, in a gesture borrowed from the part of the business world where a deal gets sealed by shaking on it. The handshake is the only emoji that makes a promise, and it makes it in the name of both people in the picture.

🤝 does at least four jobs in a text, and the drawing does not pick between them. It closes a deal. It ends an argument. It says we are in this together. It signs off a post. Three of those are commitments of some kind, and the emoji gives the reader no way to tell which one you sent.

The reference entry for the glyph lives at [/emoji/handshake](/emoji/handshake). What that page cannot hold is the part that matters: which sentence you are actually typing when you reach for it, what the handshake does in each room people use it in, and why the handshake took five years to grow a pair of hands that belong to two different people.

## The handshake was named for what it meant, not for what it shows

Most emoji are named after the thing they draw. The cat face is a cat face. The rocket is a rocket. The handshake is different: the Unicode Consortium called the character HANDSHAKE the first time it appeared, in Unicode 9.0 in November 2016, and the annotation that shipped with it that year already listed agreement among the keywords, next to handshake, meeting, shake, and hand. The deal reading was not a later Internet invention. It was in the file when the character was approved.

That is worth sitting with for a second, because it explains why the emoji behaves the way it does. The handshake did not drift into the meaning it has. It was built for it. The two hands come in from opposite sides and meet in the middle, which makes it the only gesture emoji on the keyboard that is really a two-party object. There is no version of 🤝 where one person is shaking hands alone.

The physical ritual behind it is the part that does the damage. A spoken "yeah, let's do Thursday" is an offer. "Shake on it" converts that offer into a binding thing, in front of a witness, with the word agreement attached. The emoji keeps the ritual and drops the words, which leaves the commitment with nowhere to go except into the recipient's head.

The skin tone story is where the drawing gives itself away. When the glyph arrived in 2016, both hands were the same abstract yellow, which is a polite fiction about a transaction between two unspecified people. Emoji 12.0 added the five single tones in 2019. Emoji 14.0, in 2021, added the sequence that lets the two hands wear different tones, so the handshake can finally show a light hand and a dark hand meeting in the middle. That last change is the one that matters for reading the emoji. For the first five years of its life, 🤝 could not show two different people shaking hands. Now it can, and now every sender has to pick a tone for both hands or leave the default in place, and the choice is read.

## Four sentences, one drawing

### 🤝 "We have a deal."

The literal reading, and the one the drawing was designed for. The handshake as the written form of "shaking on it".

- **"thursday works 🤝"** to a client who asked to move a deadline. You have agreed to Thursday.
- **"i'll send the deck tonight, you send the brief 🤝"** splitting a piece of work in two.
- **"we're locked in 🤝"** after a plan that everybody approved.

This is the one job where the emoji is honest, and it is also the one with a price tag. A handshake implies the terms were settled. If they were not, the emoji quietly pretends they were.

### 🤝 "We are done arguing."

The truce, and the reason the emoji ended up in so many group chats. Nothing is agreed except the fact that the argument is over.

- **"ok fine 🤝"** after thirty minutes of a thread nobody is going to win.
- **"you and me, not them 🤝"** ending a side conversation with somebody else.
- **"agreed, dropping it 🤝"** closing a topic that two people are still annoyed about.

A truce and a deal are the same drawing, and they cost different amounts. The truce version ends a conversation. The deal version commits somebody to a future action. Sending the wrong one to somebody who was counting on a commitment is how a handshake becomes a grievance.

### 🤝 "Both of us."

The equalizer, and the least obvious of the four. The handshake can put two people on the same level, which is how it gets used to share a situation or, less charmingly, to share a mistake.

- **"we both forgot 🤝"** after a meeting that was on both of you.
- **"your mess and my mess, same problem 🤝"** in a group chat.
- **"mutual 🤝"** to close a topic where nobody was the villain.

Read this one carefully before you send it. The equalizing version is warm and useful. The shared-blame version hands half of a problem to somebody who was about to apologise for all of it.

### 🤝 "Same."

The reaction, and the sign-off. The picture doing the punctuation, with no agreement attached to anything.

- **"🤝"** under a friend's post, where the emoji is the whole reply.
- **"true 🤝"** at the end of a hot take.
- **"💯🤝"** as a double sign-off on a shared win.

This is the most common use of the emoji by volume and the least risky, because nothing is being promised. It is also the use that has flattened the meaning of the glyph in a group chat: when 🤝 shows up fifty times a day as a sign-off, a 🤝 that means "we have a deal" has to fight for attention.

## The same handshake, six ways

| The message          | What it reads as                    |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| "thursday works 🤝"  | I am committing to Thursday         |
| "ok fine 🤝"         | I am done arguing, not that you won |
| "we both forgot 🤝"  | You are half of this                |
| "true 🤝"            | I agree, and I am done here         |
| "we're locked in 🤝" | Both of us are committed            |
| "🤝"                 | Same, and please stop talking       |

Six messages, one drawing, and the only thing separating a commitment from a sign-off is the sentence in front of it. That is the whole technique and the whole hazard.

## The handshake is not the applause, and it is not the briefcase

The [👏 clapping hands](/emoji/clapping-hands) is the emoji that gets confused with 🤝 most often, because both pictures show two hands coming together. They are different characters with different code points, and the applause one is the older of the pair, dating back to Emoji 1.0 in 2015. The split is about time: 👏 reports that something already happened and everybody enjoyed it, 🤝 states that something is now agreed and somebody is on the hook.

The [💼 briefcase](/emoji/briefcase) is the other neighbour people tap by accident, because it sits close to the handshake in most pickers and carries the paperwork the handshake only implies. The handshake closes the deal in the air. The briefcase is the contract and the money behind it. If somebody says "send the contract" and you answer with 🤝, you have answered a different question.

Then there are the single-person alternatives, and each one does less than the handshake because each one only moves in one direction. The [✅ check mark](/emoji/check-mark) says a task is finished. The [👍 thumbs up](/emoji/thumbs-up) approves one thing, and from a manager it is a decision while from a peer it is a vote. The [👌 ok hand](/emoji/ok-hand) means received, which is a fact about the past. The [🙏 folded hands](/emoji/folded-hands) asks for something, which is the opposite direction of power from a handshake, since one person is supplicating and the other is granting.

For the version of agreement that stays away from contracts, the [👊 oncoming fist](/emoji/oncoming-fist) is the one that fits. It is a fist bump, one gesture between two people, and it carries none of the paperwork. The [🙌 raised hands](/emoji/raising-hands) belongs to afterwards, when the thing is already done and worth celebrating.

## Where 🤝 reliably fails

1. **As a substitute for an agreement you did not make.** "thursday works 🤝" commits you to Thursday. If the date is still soft, a yes with a wiggle room does not survive the drawing.
2. **In a work channel with somebody outside your company.** It lands as closing terms that were never negotiated, and the other side may read it as a commitment their legal team did not make.
3. **To somebody you have just met.** The gesture is level in the drawing and formal in the culture. On a first exchange it reads as an older person settling a junior one.
4. **In a group chat where a third party is in the room.** You cannot shake hands on something that excludes the person reading. Two people agreeing to something that concerns a third is exactly the moment this emoji causes trouble.
5. **Mid-argument.** "fine 🤝" while somebody is still angry reads as a peace treaty you negotiated alone.
6. **To bad news.** Somebody says they lost their job and you send 🤝. The drawing has no way to be sad.
7. **As a reply to a question.** "what time? 🤝" does not answer the question. The handshake seals things, it does not ask for them.
8. **In a dating thread.** Early on, the business register lands as terms rather than warmth, and it reads faintly transactional.
9. **In email.** A handshake in a formal message is a strange object, and most professional readers will read the sender as trying to sound like a deal instead of writing one.
10. **As a way of approving somebody else's work.** Sending 🤝 about a decision that belongs to someone else puts words in their mouth, with a smile on it.
11. **Upward, when the room is not level.** The drawing is always equal. A handshake sent to a boss, a client, or a professor is not, and the emoji hides that gap instead of acknowledging it.
12. **As the entire message.** A bare 🤝 carries a promise with no terms attached, which is the version most likely to be remembered as the sender claiming more than they agreed to.

## Replies that volley back

- **Handshake it back.** Replying to a 🤝 with a 🤝 is the complete answer when the first one was a sign-off. Matching it is the point.
- **Convert the deal into a task.** When the agreement is real and work follows, [✅ the check mark](/emoji/check-mark) says done in a way that does not reopen the negotiation.
- **Acknowledge without promising.** When you have received something and nothing is expected in return, [👌 the ok hand](/emoji/ok-hand) is the reply. It is about the past, so it cannot be read as a deal.
- **Approve one specific thing.** A [👍](/emoji/thumbs-up) pins the approval to the item in front of it and takes the contract out of the message.
- **Go official.** [🤝💼 the handshake-briefcase combo](/combo/handshake-briefcase) is the version for when a deal genuinely has paperwork behind it.
- **Mark the win.** [🤝✨ the handshake-sparkles combo](/combo/handshake-sparkles-v2) says the agreement landed and it was worth it, which a plain handshake cannot.
- **Say all good.** [👍👌 the all-good combo](/combo/thumbs-up-ok) closes a small exchange with approval and acknowledgement in one tap.
- **Confirm receipt.** [👌✅ the ok-confirm combo](/combo/ok-hand-check-mark) tells the sender the message landed and was understood, which is a smaller promise than an agreement and does not read like one.
- **Confirm with a sentence.** "yes, Thursday works" is worth more than "thursday works 🤝" every single time, because nobody can quote a sentence back to you and pretend it was a contract.
- **Ask for what you need.** When the emoji should have been a request, [🙏](/emoji/folded-hands) is the right direction, and [❓](/emoji/question-mark) ends it: "can we do Thursday ❓".
- **Drop the paperwork.** When the tone has gotten too formal for the friendship, a 👊 fist bump keeps the agreement and drops the contract.

## Who reads the handshake, and where

Gen Z sends the handshake as a sign-off and as a bit. "we're locked in 🤝" is the sincere version and "🤝" under a business meme is the joke version, where the emoji is doing the eye-roll at corporate language rather than speaking it. Millennials tend to use it deliberately, usually for a real agreement or a truce, and they hear the deal in it more often than older senders do. Older senders read it the way they would read the actual gesture, as sealing something, which is why it still survives in business email threads where the newer emoji would have been deleted.

Platforms change it more than generations do. On Discord and Slack the handshake lives in the reaction bar, one tap, no words, which is the exact setup where it does the most damage: a bare 🤝 under a plan reads as approval to whoever is running the channel, whether or not the person tapping agreed to anything. On Instagram it arrives at the end of a caption, on TikTok it is the fastest possible comment, and on X it is the reply sign-off. All five are places where the sentence is short or missing, and the picture is doing all the work.

The pattern worth remembering: the handshake is only as binding as the room allows. In a group chat with friends, a bare 🤝 costs almost nothing. In a channel with a client, it can cost a deadline. When the agreement matters, type it.

## FAQ

**What does 🤝 mean in a text?**
It most often means "we have a deal" or "we agree." The same glyph ends an argument, puts two people on the same level, and signs off a post. The words in front of it decide which one you sent.

**Is 🤝 rude?**
On its own, rarely. The reading that causes real damage is sending it for an agreement the other person never made, and the reading that causes confusion is using it in a thread where somebody else was part of the deal without being part of the handshake.

**What does 🤝 mean at work?**
It means the deal is closed, and it is the only emoji that implies terms. Use it after the terms are set. Before that, it creates an agreement that nobody negotiated.

**What is the difference between 🤝 and 👏?**
👏 is applause, a reaction to something that already happened. 🤝 is an agreement, a claim about something that is about to happen. One looks backward and one looks forward.

**Should I use the skin tone versions of 🤝?**
Pick a tone when you want the handshake to show the two specific people involved, and keep the default yellow when the agreement is between people whose appearance is not the point. The tone is read, so choose it on purpose or leave it alone.

**What does 🤝 mean from a guy or a girl?**
Nothing gender-specific. The gesture is level by design, and it reads the same whoever sends it. What changes the reading is the room, not the sender.
