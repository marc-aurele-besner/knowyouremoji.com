The clay lamp is the whole picture. For most of the years people have been texting Happy Diwali, that picture was not on the keyboard, so the greeting went out in someone else's clothes. Fireworks. Sparkles. A wax candle. A fire. Those keys still stand in for the lamp in 2026, and each of them belongs to a different night.

Unicode added the diya lamp in 2019, U+1FA94, a small oil lamp with a flame, filed next to the other lights. The name of the character is the object. It does not say New Year or Christmas, and it does not announce that the sender researched your culture. It says lamp. Families had already been typing the greeting since the first smartphones, and the nearest keys were a sky explosion, a glitter mark, and the candle you blow out on a cake.

This year's lamp night is Sunday, November 8. The texts start earlier than that. Dhanteras, the shopping day that opens the festival in many Hindu homes, is Friday, November 6. The lunar calendar is awkward in 2026: Naraka Chaturdashi lands on that same Sunday instead of getting a day of its own, Govardhan Puja waits until Tuesday, November 10, and Bhai Dooj, the sibling day, is Wednesday, November 11. Local calendars nudge the edges. Sunday night does not move. The new-moon day also runs into Monday, which is why a Monday-morning greeting is still on time and a text on November 20 is not.

## The years the greeting had to borrow

From the first emoji keyboards until 2019, a Diwali text had to point at a different object and hope the sentence corrected it.

- **🎆 Fireworks** have been a sky show since the 2010 set. New Year's Eve, a stadium, a city that still lights crackers. Nothing in the glyph says lamp. The page is [fireworks](/emoji/fireworks).
- **🎇 The sparkler** is the same era, a stick in someone's hand. [The sparkler](/emoji/sparkler) is a fuse you are holding.
- **✨ Sparkles** are decoration with no object attached. They make a sentence look special and never say which occasion. The [sparkles guide](/guides/what-does-sparkles-emoji-mean) is the longer version, and the key lives at [sparkles](/emoji/sparkles).
- **🕯️ The candle** arrived in 2014, Unicode 7.0, U+1F56F. Wax, a wick, a birthday, a vigil. [The candle](/emoji/candle) is the small flame people grab when the lamp is not in their recents.
- **🔥 Fire** is heat, a warning, or a compliment that has gotten cheap. [The fire guide](/guides/what-does-fire-emoji-mean) covers the compliment. On a lamp festival it is the wrong temperature. The page is [fire](/emoji/fire).
- **🎉 The party popper** is a party that could be anything. [The celebration cheat sheet](/guides/celebration-emoji-cheat-sheet-good-news) is where that key belongs. A promotion wears it better than a Diwali greeting.

People sent those because the lamp was missing, not because a diya is a firework. The habit outlived the gap. The new key sits a few scrolls away from the fire, and a lot of family chats never went looking. A sticker pack from 2018 still works. Three people can celebrate one night and send three other holidays.

Two religious keys get dragged in for the same reason, and they are worse borrows. The om symbol was on phones years before the lamp. It is a sacred syllable. Pasting it on a card does not make the card more careful. [The om](/emoji/om) page is that syllable. The lotus flower arrived in 2021, later than the lamp, and on a phone it often reads as a studio logo. [The lotus](/emoji/lotus) is the flower. [The lotus position](/emoji/lotus-position) is a person sitting down. Neither one lights a house.

## Which picture is actually in the text

| "deepavali 🪔"                                | The same night, in the name that house uses                        |
| "the balcony is lit 🪔🪔🪔"                   | A photograph of real lamps, not a card                             |
| "happy diwali 🎆"                             | They mean the festival. They sent New Year's                       |
| "happy diwali ✨🎉"                           | A card. Warm, and interchangeable with any other sparkly week      |
| "happy diwali 🕯️"                             | A small flame. The wax candle, not the clay lamp                   |
| "happy diwali 🔥"                             | A compliment, or a warning, parked on the wrong night              |
| "dhanteras, the utensils are in 🪔" on Nov. 6 | The festival has opened. It is not Sunday yet                      |
| "bhai dooj 🪔" on November 11                 | The sibling day. The lamp is still in season. The occasion changed |

Read the object before you read the warmth. A few clusters show up every year.

**The greeting.** One lamp and the words. "Happy Diwali 🪔" is the text that survives a family chat with three generations in it. The entry itself is [the diya lamp](/emoji/diya-lamp). Stacking lamps is the balcony: one is hello, a row is the house.

**The name.** A lot of North Indian and diaspora chats say Diwali. Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam, and Kannada households, and a lot of families in Singapore, Malaysia, and Sri Lanka, say Deepavali. The lamp does not pick a side. The word does. If you already know which word that house uses, use it. If you do not, "wishing you light this week 🪔" is safer than a confident guess at the name. Correcting someone else's name for their own holiday is worse than either choice.

**The borrowed sky.** Fireworks and sparklers. In a lot of Indian cities, and in a lot of family chats that have already had the argument, crackers are a fight about air, animals, and kids. 🎆 takes a side in that fight. The lamp does not. If the thread has been tense about noise, the burst is a vote, and the [sparkler-and-fireworks combo](/combo/sparkler-firework) is a louder vote. Save the sky for a person who is actually talking about a display.

**The glitter card.** Sparkles plus a party popper is what a brand sends when it has a calendar and no relationship. From a sibling it is thin. From a manager who scheduled over the evening, it is a card taped to the meeting. [Fireworks and sparkles](/combo/fireworks-sparkles) is the same card with more sky. Its ordinary job is a countdown or a launch, and it keeps that job unless the words are very clear.

**The other holidays in the same scroll.** In the United States this week already belongs to early Christmas trees and, a bit later, to Thanksgiving. A mixed family chat in November can hold 🎄, 🦃, and 🪔 in one afternoon. Answer the one they sent. [The Christmas guide](/guides/christmas-emoji-etiquette) is the tree. [The Thanksgiving guide](/guides/thanksgiving-emoji-etiquette) is the turkey. Neither one is a lamp, and a rainbow is a third holiday. Holi is the color festival, in the spring. 🌈 on a Diwali text is the wrong month.

**Not every house is marking the same night.** Hindu Lakshmi Puja is the version most Happy Diwali cards assume. It is not the only observance on this date. Bandi Chhor Divas, observed by Sikhs, marks Guru Hargobind's release from Gwalior Fort and falls on this day. It is its own commemoration, not a nickname for the puja. A lamp can be welcome there. An om symbol is a different religion's syllable, and it does not belong in that text unless that family uses it. For many Jains the night marks Mahavira's nirvana: lamps, yes, a wish that someone get rich, no. In Nepal the overlapping days are Tihar. The lamps are still the point, and the sequence of days is that house's sequence. If you do not know which of these you were invited into, send the lamp and the words, and leave the theology in the sentence they already have.

People who learned emoji as little drawings will see a firework as a firework until the sentence teaches them otherwise. A phone old enough to have missed the 2019 update may show the lamp as an empty box, and then the words are the entire greeting. The [parent emoji dictionary](/guides/parents-emoji-dictionary-is-from-2014) is the longer map of that gap. If you cannot count on the picture arriving, type the holiday.

## Where the lamp flips on you

1. **A bare 🪔.** No words, sent to someone who has never celebrated. They may see a small flame they cannot place. "Happy Diwali" or "Happy Deepavali" does the translation. The picture will not.
2. **The pamphlet.** "Happy Diwali 🪔, the festival of lights, good over evil 🕉️🪷" explains a person's holiday to them. They have the festival. You have a search result. The om and the lotus are the part that lectures.
3. **Fire as a greeting.** "happy diwali 🔥" reads as "you look good," "this is dangerous," or "I am hyped." None of those is the night. Use the lamp.
4. **A candle in a hard year.** Wax candles already mean a vigil in a lot of chats. If you are not sure this house is celebrating, because of a death, a move, or a year they have quietly opted out, a candle is a bad guess and a lamp copied from a brand account is not much kinder. A sentence asks. An emoji announces.
5. **Crackers, again.** "lighting them at 9 🎆" in a thread that asked people not to. You did not send a greeting. You sent a plan.
6. **The work calendar.** A 🪔 and "Happy Diwali" in a channel where people celebrate is an ordinary greeting. A 🪔 from a company that booked the evening anyway is not a greeting. The sentence that matters is about the time. The emoji cannot grant the evening off.
7. **Sweets that are the wrong sweet.** There is no laddoo on the keyboard. 🍬 is a wrapped candy from a different shop, and a cookie is worse. If the box is the point, say so in words.
8. **The late card.** Monday morning still lands. November 20 does not. If you missed the week, say you missed it.

## Replies that name the right night

- **They sent 🪔.** One lamp back is the whole conversation. Add the word they used, Diwali or Deepavali, if you know it. Do not upgrade their lamp into a row of religious symbols.
- **They sent fireworks or glitter, and you know they mean this holiday.** Answer with the lamp, so the thread gets the object. Matching 🎆 teaches the chat that the sky show was correct.
- **They are actually talking about a display.** Then the sky is honest. [The sparkler and the burst](/combo/sparkler-firework) matches a person who means crackers or a show. It is a bad match for an aunt who meant lamps and grabbed the nearest bright key.
- **The message is thanks, not the greeting.** They hosted, they sent a box, they covered a shift so someone else could be home. [Grateful thanks](/combo/pray-thanks) is folded hands plus sparkles: the thanks is the hands, and the shine is the same generic sparkle that makes cards look like cards. It fits a real favor. It is a strange way to say happy Diwali with nothing else attached. [Folded hands](/emoji/folded-hands) alone is the older key, and [the folded-hands guide](/guides/what-does-folded-hands-mean) is why it splits: prayer, thanks, or a high-five, depending on the phone and the person. On this week a lot of readers will take it as the greeting. If you mean thanks, put the thanks in the sentence.
- **You are not sure they celebrate.** Wait for their lamp, or write a sentence with no picture. A googled 🪔 sent just in case, to a colleague, is a guess about their religion. The guess is the part that can go wrong.
- **Wednesday, November 11.** That is Bhai Dooj on the calendar most of North India is using, also called Bhau Beej or Bhai Phonta in other houses. The text is about the sibling. A generic "happy diwali" on the sibling day reads as if you lost count. Use their name for the day if you know it. The lamp can stay. The occasion is no longer Sunday.

Before you send a picture this week, name the object out loud. A lamp means the festival. A burst means the sky. Glitter means a card. A sacred syllable means you are saying a sacred syllable. The person on the other side can only tell those apart if you can.

## FAQ

**What emoji do you send for Diwali?**

🪔, the diya lamp, plus the words. One lamp is the greeting. A row of lamps is a photo of the house. Fireworks, sparkles, a party popper, a candle, and a fire are older keys that got used because the lamp was not there yet.

**When should the 2026 text go out?**

The lamp night is Sunday, November 8. Friday, November 6 is Dhanteras for many Hindu homes, so a shopping text that day is early on purpose. Monday morning is still inside the new-moon day. Wednesday, November 11 is the sibling day, and it wants its own name. A text on November 20 is late.

**Are fireworks wrong?**

They are the wrong object for a lamp greeting, and in a thread that has already argued about crackers they are a vote. They are the right object when someone is actually talking about a display. The sentence has to say which of those you mean, because the picture is a sky show either way.

**Should the text say Diwali or Deepavali?**

Whichever that house says. The lamp is the same. The word is how they can tell you were listening to them.

**Someone sent an om symbol. What is that doing there?**

It is a sacred syllable, not a decoration for the lamps. In a Hindu family that uses it, it may be sincere. In a work channel, a brand blast, or a Sikh observance of Bandi Chhor Divas, it is the wrong insertion. Reply to the greeting they made, and do not add another religion's symbol to be thorough.
